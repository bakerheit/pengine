# Probable Cause — Vehicle Lore

This is the in-world history for the vehicle makers that currently matter to
the story and the road. It records creative direction, not a real-world brand
history or a promise that every company has a playable lineup. For the current
catalog, model names, and badge-production status, see
[Vehicle Brands](../design/vehicle-brands.md).

## BWC — Belgium Working Coach

BWC began in 1879 as a Belgian maker of hard-wearing horse-drawn coaches for
tradespeople, municipal services, and rural routes. The company did not begin
as a luxury carriage house. Its reputation came from making a coach that could
be repaired with ordinary tools after a bad road, a winter, or a careless load.

Its first motorized working coach reached customers in **1908**. It was a
short-wheelbase delivery vehicle with a simple one-cylinder engine, exposed
service points, and coachwork built by the same people who had made its carts.
The first true BWC passenger car followed in **1914**. It used the same
practical idea: upright seating, easy access to the mechanical parts, and
enough clearance to survive roads outside the city.

In **1923**, BWC introduced its first dedicated motor-truck range. The trucks
made the name useful beyond Belgium's towns and cemented the company's fleet
identity. After the Second World War, BWC concentrated on compact family cars,
delivery vans, buses, and municipal work vehicles. It became the brand a small
business buys when it needs one vehicle to do every job without drama.

Early BWC engineers are remembered for three modest innovations rather than a
single miracle invention:

- a sealed ignition cover that kept a wet road from becoming a stranded day;
- replaceable wheel-hub and brake parts that could be changed individually;
- leaf-spring packs built from common sections, so local workshops could repair
  a working vehicle without ordering an entire axle.

That history informs the modern name. BWC cars should feel purposeful,
overbuilt, and faintly pleased with themselves. The current curved-letter
roundel borrows the seriousness of a German prestige badge but uses Belgian
black, yellow, and red. It is fitted to the **360**, a compact four-door sedan
with a planted rear-drive road tune. **Krammeit** remains only a possible
future model name.

## GLM — General Lifetime Motors

GLM was founded in the United States in 1925 during a wave of industrial
consolidation. It took the language of permanence seriously: the name promises
that the company will outlive a car, its owner, and probably the town where it
was bought. That is why its public identity is clipped, corporate, and a little
soulless.

The early business made ordinary family vehicles and fleet sedans. Its design
culture was built around common parts, wide dealer coverage, and annual
revisions that looked larger than they were. GLM is not remembered for a
single breakthrough safety invention. Its reputation is for scale: it gets
reliable machinery into every kind of driveway, then lets the marketing
department call the result progress.

The contradiction is its performance division. Every so often GLM approves a
car that escapes the committee process: dramatic, fast, and far less sensible
than the brand's paperwork. The **Lunge** and **Zip** belong to that side of
the company. A GLM performance car should feel like a corporate expense report
that somehow became fun.

The old internal identity, GLR, is no longer current. In new writing, use
**GLM** and expand it as **General Lifetime Motors**.

## Spagatti

Spagatti was founded in Italy in 1950 by a small postwar grand-touring concern.
It sold romance as much as engineering: long drives, expensive lunches, a loud
arrival, and the belief that a fast car can make any owner look more
interesting. The company was never the biggest Italian maker, which is exactly
why it doubled down on presentation.

Its early cars earned attention for low bodies, dramatic proportions, and
interiors that made a modest cabin feel ceremonial. Spagatti's racing history
is intentionally uneven. It has enough old photographs, club trophies, and
half-remembered runs to feed the legend, but not a clean list of championships
that settles the argument.

By the later twentieth century, the company had shifted from small sports cars
to expensive grand tourers. The **Shū** carries that attitude: exotic, polished,
and a little absurd on purpose. Spagatti's joke works best when the car still
looks genuinely desirable. The brand should never become a disposable gag.

## Rodeo

Rodeo was founded in the United States in 1965 to build trucks for owners who
worked on rough ground but still had to drive home on pavement. Its early
success came from simple pickups with usable beds, tall suspension, and
mechanicals a rural shop could understand. The name draws on ranch country,
but the brand is not costume western: its customers include farmers,
contractors, utility crews, and people who simply need a vehicle that can take
a bad road without an apology.

In the 1970s, Rodeo made four-wheel drive and long-travel suspension central to
its identity. The company became known for truck frames that outlasted their
first paint and for owner manuals written like practical advice instead of
sales copy. Later family 4x4s widened the audience without changing that
character.

The **Grazer 4x4** is the present expression of Rodeo: broad-shouldered,
work-ready, and happy to look slightly too tough in a grocery-store parking
lot. Its black-and-orange branding-iron R is fitted to the grille and tailgate.
See the [Grazer emblem record](../assets/rodeo-grazer-emblem.md) for the
implemented badge and evidence.

## Still open

The four histories above establish the companies' public stories and design
language. They do not establish founder names, exact factory towns, parent
companies, racing results, dealership chains, or current executives. Add those
only when a mission, location, or vehicle needs them. A brand should gain
history through the places and people who use it, not through a fake corporate
encyclopedia.
